# Belief

**Category:** epistemology

## Definition

Belief is a psychological attitude of accepting or holding a proposition to be true, regardless of whether it actually is true or whether one has adequate justification for it.

## Description

Belief is the most basic epistemic attitude—the foundation upon which knowledge, opinion, and doubt are built. To believe something is to mentally assent to it, to regard it as true or likely true. Belief can exist without knowledge (I can believe false things) and without adequate justification (I can have irrational beliefs), but it is traditionally considered necessary for knowledge.

Understanding belief is crucial for epistemology because most of our cognitive lives involve evaluating, forming, and revising beliefs rather than achieving certain knowledge.

## Characteristics of Belief

### Propositional Content

Beliefs have content that can be expressed as propositions:

- "I believe that Paris is in France"
- "She believes it will rain tomorrow"
- The content can be true or false, simple or complex

### Degrees of Confidence

Beliefs come in varying strengths:

- **Certain belief**: Complete confidence (rare)
- **Strong belief**: High confidence
- **Tentative belief**: Moderate confidence
- **Weak belief**: Low but positive confidence

### Involuntary Nature

We cannot directly control our beliefs:

- You cannot simply decide to believe something you find unconvincing
- Beliefs respond to evidence and reasons, not mere will
- This raises questions about moral and epistemic responsibility for beliefs

## Belief vs Other Attitudes

### Belief vs [Knowledge](knowledge.md)

- **Knowledge**: Justified true belief (traditionally)
- **Belief**: Can be unjustified, false, or both
- All knowledge involves belief, but not all belief constitutes knowledge

### Belief vs Opinion

- **Opinion**: Often suggests uncertainty or subjectivity
- **Belief**: Can be held with great confidence
- The distinction may be more social than philosophical

### Belief vs Assumption

- **Assumption**: Accepted for purposes of argument or action
- **Belief**: Involves actual psychological commitment
- You can assume things you don't believe

### Belief vs Acceptance

- **Acceptance**: Taking something as true for practical purposes
- **Belief**: Involves conviction about truth
- You might accept a theory without believing it's true

## Types of Beliefs

### Basic vs Non-Basic Beliefs

- **Basic beliefs**: Not based on other beliefs (perceptual beliefs, memory beliefs)
- **Non-basic beliefs**: Derived from other beliefs through reasoning

### Conscious vs Unconscious Beliefs

- **Conscious beliefs**: Explicitly held and readily accessible
- **Unconscious beliefs**: Implicit assumptions that guide behavior

### Occurrent vs Dispositional Beliefs

- **Occurrent beliefs**: Currently being entertained mentally
- **Dispositional beliefs**: Would be affirmed if considered (stored beliefs)

## Formation of Beliefs

### Perception

Direct sensory experience forms basic perceptual beliefs

- "I see a red car"
- Foundation for much empirical belief
- Subject to error and interpretation

### Memory

Past experiences preserved as belief

- "I remember eating breakfast"
- Reconstructive rather than purely reproductive
- Can create false memories

### Testimony

Beliefs formed from others' reports

- Most beliefs come from testimony
- Requires assessment of source reliability
- Social dimension of belief formation

### Reasoning

[Deductive](../logic/deduction.md) and [inductive](induction.md) processes

- Drawing conclusions from other beliefs
- Can amplify or correct initial beliefs
- Subject to logical errors and biases

## Justification of Beliefs

### Evidential Justification

Beliefs supported by good evidence

- Empirical evidence from experience
- Testimonial evidence from reliable sources
- Logical evidence from valid reasoning

### Coherential Justification  

Beliefs that fit well with other accepted beliefs

- Internal consistency of belief system
- Mutual support among beliefs
- No isolated foundational beliefs

### Pragmatic Justification

Beliefs justified by their practical consequences

- William James's "will to believe"
- Useful for action and well-being
- Controversial as genuine justification

## Rationality and Belief

### Epistemic Rationality

Beliefs aimed at truth:

- Proportion belief to evidence
- Update beliefs based on new information
- Maintain logical consistency

### Practical Rationality  

Beliefs that serve goals and values:

- May sometimes conflict with epistemic rationality
- "Noble lies" and self-deception
- Role of emotion and motivation

## Problems with Belief

### Confirmation Bias

Tendency to seek information confirming existing beliefs

- Selective attention to supportive evidence
- Dismissal of contrary evidence
- Maintains false beliefs

### Belief Perseverance

Beliefs persist even after supporting evidence is discredited

- Psychological investment in beliefs
- Identity formation around beliefs
- Social pressure to maintain beliefs

### Motivated Reasoning

Reasoning influenced by what we want to believe

- Unconscious bias in evidence evaluation
- Rationalization of desired conclusions
- Conflict between truth and other values

## Belief and Action

### Belief-Desire Model

Actions explained by beliefs and desires:

- Beliefs about means-ends relationships
- Desires for particular outcomes
- Rational action follows from both

### Pragmatic Encroachment

Practical stakes can affect knowledge and justified belief:

- Higher stakes require stronger evidence
- Context affects epistemic standards
- Challenges traditional knowledge/action distinction

## Doxastic Attitudes (Belief States)

### Belief (Positive)

Accepting proposition as true

- Confidence above neutral threshold
- Commitment to truth of proposition

### Disbelief (Negative)

Rejecting proposition as false

- Confidence below neutral threshold
- Commitment to falsity of proposition

### Suspension of Judgment (Neutral)

Neither believing nor disbelieving

- Agnostic position
- Insufficient evidence either way
- Deliberate withholding of judgment

## Significance for A Fortiori

Understanding belief is crucial for philosophical analysis. When evaluating arguments, we must distinguish between what we believe, what we know, and what we merely assume. The psychology of belief affects how we process evidence and form judgments about philosophical questions.

## Key Questions

- Is belief voluntary or involuntary?
- What degree of evidence is required for rational belief?
- Can we have moral obligations regarding our beliefs?
- How do social factors influence individual beliefs?
