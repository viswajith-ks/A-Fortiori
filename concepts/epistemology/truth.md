# Truth

**Category:** epistemology

## Definition
Truth is the property of statements, beliefs, or propositions that corresponds to reality or facts about the world.

## Description
Truth is one of the most fundamental concepts in philosophy, yet one of the most contested. What makes a statement true? How can we recognize truth? Are there different kinds of truth? These questions have occupied philosophers for millennia and remain actively debated.

The concept of truth is essential to knowledge, since knowledge traditionally requires true belief. It's also central to logic, science, ethics, and everyday discourse. Yet despite its fundamental importance, philosophers disagree about truth's nature.

## Major Theories of Truth

### Correspondence Theory
**Core Idea**: A statement is true if it corresponds to reality.
- "Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white
- Truth involves a relationship between statements and facts
- **Strengths**: Intuitive, aligns with common sense
- **Problems**: What are "facts"? How does correspondence work?

**Versions:**
- **Classical**: Statements mirror reality
- **Structural**: Statements share structure with reality  
- **Causal**: True beliefs are caused by the facts they represent

### Coherence Theory
**Core Idea**: A statement is true if it coheres with other accepted beliefs.
- Truth emerges from consistency within a belief system
- No single statement is true in isolation
- **Strengths**: Handles theoretical knowledge well
- **Problems**: Competing coherent systems; possible disconnection from reality

### Pragmatic Theory
**Core Idea**: A statement is true if believing it has successful practical consequences.
- Truth is defined by utility rather than correspondence
- Associated with William James and John Dewey
- **Strengths**: Connects truth to human purposes
- **Problems**: False beliefs can sometimes be useful

### Deflationary Theories
**Core Idea**: "True" adds nothing substantial to statements.
- "Snow is white is true" means the same as "Snow is white"
- Truth is not a substantial property but a linguistic device
- **Versions**: Redundancy theory, disquotational theory, minimalist theory
- **Strengths**: Avoids metaphysical puzzles
- **Problems**: Seems to miss something important about truth

## Types of Truth Claims

### Empirical Truths
Claims about the observable world
- "Water boils at 100°C at sea level"
- Testable through experience and experiment
- Subject to revision based on new evidence

### Logical/Mathematical Truths
Claims that seem necessarily true
- "2 + 2 = 4"
- "All bachelors are unmarried"
- Independent of empirical observation?

### Moral Truths
Claims about right and wrong
- "Murder is wrong"
- Status as truths heavily disputed
- Connection to facts unclear

### Religious/Metaphysical Truths
Claims about ultimate reality
- "God exists"
- "Everything is material"
- Often beyond empirical verification

## Truth and Knowledge
Truth is traditionally required for [Knowledge](knowledge.md):
- You cannot know false propositions
- Knowledge requires justified true belief (though [Gettier problems](knowledge.md) complicate this)
- The [Truth](truth.md) condition distinguishes knowledge from mere belief

## Skeptical Challenges to Truth

### Relativism
- Truth varies by culture, historical period, or individual
- "True for you but not for me"
- **Challenge**: If relativism is true, is that truth relative?

### Anti-Realism
- Truth is constructed rather than discovered
- Reality is mind-dependent or language-dependent
- **Challenge**: Consistency problems and practical implausibility

## Truth and Logic
Truth is central to logical evaluation:
- [Valid](../logic/validity.md) arguments preserve truth from premises to conclusion
- [Sound](../logic/soundness.md) arguments have true premises and valid form
- Logical systems are designed to track truth relationships

## Truth Conditions vs Truth Values

### Truth Conditions
The conditions under which a statement would be true
- "The cat is on the mat" is true if the cat is on the mat
- Specifies what reality must be like for truth

### Truth Values  
Whether a statement is actually true or false
- Most statements have definite truth values
- Some may be neither true nor false (vagueness, future contingents)

## Contemporary Debates

### Truth-Makers
What in reality makes statements true?
- States of affairs, facts, tropes, objects?
- How specific must truth-makers be?

### Truth Pluralism
Different types of statements may require different theories of truth
- Scientific statements: correspondence
- Moral statements: coherence
- Mathematical statements: deflationary

### Truth and Meaning
Does meaning determine truth conditions or vice versa?
- Understanding meaning requires grasping truth conditions
- But truth conditions may depend on meanings

## Practical Implications
Theories of truth have consequences for:
- **Science**: How do we evaluate competing theories?
- **Law**: What makes testimony reliable?
- **Ethics**: Are moral judgments objectively true or false?
- **Politics**: How do we evaluate competing claims about policy?
