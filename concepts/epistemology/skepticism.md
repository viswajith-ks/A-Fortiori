# Skepticism

**Category:** epistemology

## Definition

Skepticism is the philosophical position that knowledge is impossible, or that we should withhold judgment about claims for which we lack adequate evidence.

## Description

Skepticism has been a driving force in philosophy since ancient times, challenging our confidence in what we think we know. Rather than a purely negative doctrine, skepticism serves important functions: it clarifies the conditions required for knowledge, exposes hidden assumptions, and motivates more careful reasoning.

For someone taking an agnostic approach to philosophical questions, skepticism provides tools for careful evaluation of claims while avoiding premature commitment to particular positions.

## Types of Skepticism

### Global vs Local Skepticism

- **Global Skepticism**: Challenges all knowledge claims
  - "We can't know anything"
  - Includes skepticism about logic, perception, memory, etc.
  - Potentially self-refuting
- **Local Skepticism**: Challenges specific domains
  - External world skepticism
  - Other minds skepticism
  - Moral skepticism
  - More defensible and focused

### Methodological vs Philosophical Skepticism

- **Methodological Skepticism**: Temporary doubt for inquiry
  - Descartes' method of doubt
  - Tool for discovering certain foundations
  - Aims to overcome skepticism eventually
- **Philosophical Skepticism**: Substantive position about knowledge
  - Genuine doubt about possibility of knowledge
  - May be permanent position

### Academic vs Pyrrhonian Skepticism

- **Academic Skepticism**: Nothing can be known (negative dogma)
  - Associated with later Platonic Academy
  - Makes positive claim about impossibility of knowledge
- **Pyrrhonian Skepticism**: Suspension of judgment (*epoché*)
  - Associated with Sextus Empiricus
  - Avoids positive claims about knowledge
  - Seeks mental tranquility (*ataraxia*)

## Classical Skeptical Arguments

### The Problem of Criterion (Sextus Empiricus)

1. To know something, we need a criterion of truth
2. To establish a criterion of truth, we need to know it's reliable
3. But this requires a criterion of truth (circular)
4. Therefore, we cannot establish any criterion of truth

### Agrippan Trilemma (Five Modes)

Any attempt at justification faces:

1. **Infinite Regress**: Every justification requires further justification
2. **Circular Reasoning**: Eventually refers back to what needs justification
3. **Arbitrary Stopping**: Accepts some claims without justification
4. **Disagreement**: Experts disagree on fundamental questions
5. **Relativity**: Appearances vary by perceiver and context

4 and 5 are additional

## Modern Skeptical Scenarios

### Cartesian Demon

- Evil demon deceives us about everything
- All sensory experience could be illusion
- How can we rule out this possibility?

### Brain in a Vat

- Contemporary version of Cartesian demon
- Brains kept alive and stimulated to produce experiences
- Matrix-like scenario of simulated reality

### [Dreaming Argument](../../arguments/descartes-dreaming-argument.md)

- How do we know we're not dreaming now?
- Dreams can seem completely realistic
- No definitive marks distinguish waking from dreaming

## Responses to Skepticism

### Foundationalism

Establish indubitable foundation beliefs

- Descartes: *cogito ergo sum*
- Basic beliefs requiring no further justification
- **Problem**: Finding genuinely indubitable beliefs

### Coherentism  

Knowledge emerges from coherent belief systems

- No foundational beliefs needed
- Mutual support among beliefs provides justification
- **Problem**: Coherent systems can be false

### Reliabilism

Focus on reliable belief-forming processes

- Knowledge doesn't require elimination of skeptical scenarios
- Reliable processes sufficient for knowledge
- **Problem**: Distinguishes reliable from unreliable processes

### Contextualism

Standards for knowledge vary by context

- In ordinary contexts, skeptical scenarios irrelevant
- High standards appropriate for philosophical contexts
- **Problem**: Captures ordinary usage but may avoid hard questions

### Moorean Response

Common sense more certain than skeptical arguments

- "Here is one hand" (G.E. Moore)
- Ordinary beliefs more credible than philosophical arguments
- **Problem**: Seems to beg the question against skeptic

## Varieties of Domain-Specific Skepticism

### External World Skepticism

Can we know anything about reality beyond our minds?

- All we directly access are mental states
- External world inferred, not directly known
- Permanent possibility of systematic deception

### Other Minds Skepticism

Can we know that others have mental states?

- Only observe behavior, not mental states directly
- Philosophical zombies seem conceivable
- Problem of analogical reasoning from single case

### Inductive Skepticism ([Hume's Problem](../../arguments/problem-of-induction.md))

Can we know that future will resemble past?

- All inductive reasoning assumes uniformity of nature
- This assumption cannot be justified non-circularly
- Science depends on induction

### Moral Skepticism

Can there be objective moral knowledge?

- Moral disagreement across cultures and times
- No clear test for moral truth
- Relationship between facts and values unclear

## Benefits of Skepticism

### Intellectual Humility

Recognizing limits of human knowledge

- Avoiding overconfidence in beliefs
- Openness to revision and new evidence
- Appropriate caution in judgment

### Critical Thinking

Demanding higher standards of evidence

- Examining assumptions and presuppositions
- Distinguishing knowledge from mere belief
- Evaluating source credibility

### Philosophical Progress

Clarifying conditions for knowledge

- Making explicit what knowledge requires
- Refining concepts through challenge
- Motivating better theories and arguments

## Dangers of Skepticism

### Paralysis

Inability to act without certain knowledge

- Practical life requires assumptions and beliefs
- Complete suspension of judgment impractical
- Risk of nihilism or despair

### Self-Refutation

Skeptical claims may undermine themselves

- "No one can know anything" seems to be knowledge claim
- Pyrrhonian suspension may avoid this problem
- But total suspension may be impossible

## Skepticism and Agnosticism

**Connections:**

- Both involve suspension of judgment
- Both recognize limitations of human knowledge
- Both avoid premature closure on difficult questions

**Differences:**

- Skepticism often broader and more systematic
- Agnosticism typically focused on specific domains (usually religious)
- Agnosticism may be more practical, less philosophical
