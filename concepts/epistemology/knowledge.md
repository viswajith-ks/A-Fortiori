# Knowledge

**Category:** epistemology

## Definition
Knowledge is commonly defined as justified true belief, though this definition faces significant philosophical challenges known as the Gettier problems.

## Description
The nature of knowledge has been central to philosophy since ancient times. What distinguishes genuine knowledge from mere opinion, belief, or lucky guesses? The traditional analysis holds that knowledge requires three necessary conditions: truth, belief, and justification. However, Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper demonstrated cases where all three conditions are met yet we intuitively deny that knowledge exists.

This problem has generated extensive debate about the nature of knowledge and whether the traditional definition can be repaired or should be abandoned entirely.

## The Traditional Definition (JTB)

For person S to know proposition P, three conditions must be met:
1. **P is true** ([Truth](truth.md) condition)
2. **S believes P** ([Belief](belief.md) condition)  
3. **S is justified in believing P** ([Justification](justification.md) ⚠️ Placeholder condition)

### Truth Condition
Knowledge requires correspondence with reality. False beliefs, no matter how justified or firmly held, cannot constitute knowledge.

### Belief Condition  
Knowledge implies belief—you cannot know something you don't believe. However, belief doesn't require knowledge (you can believe false things).

### Justification Condition
Knowledge requires reasons or evidence that support the belief. Mere lucky guesses, even if true and believed, don't count as knowledge.

## The Gettier Problems

Edmund Gettier presented counterexamples where justified true belief fails to constitute knowledge:

### Classic Gettier Case
- Smith believes "Jones will get the job and Jones has 10 coins in his pocket"
- Smith's evidence: Company president told him Jones would get the job; Smith counted 10 coins in Jones's pocket
- Reality: Smith gets the job (not Jones), but Smith has 10 coins in his pocket
- Result: Smith's belief "The person who gets the job has 10 coins" is true, believed, and justified—but not knowledge

The problem: Smith's justification doesn't properly connect to what makes his belief true.

## Responses to Gettier

### No-False-Lemmas Response
Add fourth condition: The justification must not depend on any false beliefs.  
**Problem**: Some Gettier cases don't involve false lemmas.

### Causal Theory of Knowledge
Knowledge requires appropriate causal connection between fact and belief.  
**Problem**: Abstract knowledge (mathematics) lacks clear causal connections.

### Reliabilism
Replace justification with reliability: Belief must be formed by reliable cognitive processes.  
**Problem**: Reliable processes can still produce Gettier cases.

### Virtue Epistemology
Knowledge requires intellectual virtues (careful observation, sound reasoning, etc.).  
**Problem**: Virtuous believers can still encounter Gettier problems.

### Contextualism
Standards for knowledge vary by context—what counts as knowledge depends on practical stakes and alternatives considered.

## Types of Knowledge

### Propositional Knowledge (Knowing That)
Knowledge of facts: "I know that Paris is in France"
- Subject of most epistemological analysis
- Expressed in declarative sentences

### Procedural Knowledge (Knowing How) 
Knowledge of skills: "I know how to ride a bicycle"
- May not require propositional knowledge
- Often tacit and hard to articulate

### Acquaintance Knowledge (Knowing Of)
Direct experiential knowledge: "I know Paris" (have visited)
- Immediate, not mediated by propositions
- Basis for other types of knowledge

## Sources of Knowledge

### [Perception](perception.md) ⚠️ Placeholder
Knowledge through the five senses
- Most basic source of empirical knowledge
- Subject to illusions and hallucinations

### Memory
Retention and recall of past knowledge
- Preserves knowledge over time  
- Can be unreliable or reconstructive

### Testimony
Knowledge from others' reports
- Most human knowledge comes from testimony
- Requires assessing source credibility

### Reasoning
[Deductive](../logic/deduction.md) and [inductive](induction.md) ⚠️ Placeholder inference
- Extends knowledge beyond immediate experience
- Subject to logical errors and biases

### Intuition  
Immediate intellectual insight
- Controversial as genuine knowledge source
- May be disguised reasoning or memory

## Skeptical Challenges

### Global [Skepticism](skepticism.md)
Challenges the possibility of any knowledge
- Cartesian demon scenarios
- Brain-in-vat hypotheses

### Local Skepticism
Challenges specific domains of knowledge
- External world skepticism
- Other minds skepticism
- Moral knowledge skepticism

## Contemporary Approaches

### Externalism vs Internalism
- **Externalism**: Factors outside the subject's awareness can contribute to knowledge
- **Internalism**: Only factors accessible to the subject's consciousness matter

### Foundationalism vs Coherentism
- **Foundationalism**: Knowledge rests on basic, self-evident foundations
- **Coherentism**: Knowledge emerges from mutually supporting web of beliefs

## Significance for A Fortiori
Understanding knowledge is crucial for evaluating philosophical arguments. When analyzing claims, we must ask: Is this knowledge or mere belief? What justifies these premises? How reliable are our sources of information?

## Key Questions
- Can the Gettier problem be solved, or should we abandon the JTB analysis?
- What is the relationship between knowledge and certainty?
- Is all knowledge ultimately based on faith or assumptions?
- How much knowledge is humanly possible?
