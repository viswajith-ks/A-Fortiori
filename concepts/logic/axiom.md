# Axiom

**Category:** meta-philosophy/logic

## Definition
An axiom is a statement that is taken to be true without proof, serving as a premise or starting point for further reasoning and arguments.

## Description
The word "axiom" comes from the Ancient Greek *axíōma*, meaning "that which is thought worthy or fit" or "that which commends itself as evident." Axioms are foundational propositions that cannot be deduced from any other proposition within a given system. They serve as the bedrock upon which logical systems and mathematical theories are constructed.

In classical philosophy, an axiom was considered so evident or well-established that it was accepted without controversy. In modern logic, axioms are formal starting points for reasoning, whether or not they are self-evident.

## Key Features
- **Indemonstrable**: Cannot be proven from other statements within the system
- **Foundational**: Serve as starting points for deriving other truths
- **Context-dependent**: What counts as axiomatic varies by logical system
- **Self-evident or stipulated**: May be obviously true or simply assumed for the system

## Types of Axioms
- **Logical axioms**: Universal principles of reasoning (e.g., Law of Non-contradiction)
- **Non-logical axioms**: Domain-specific assumptions (e.g., mathematical postulates)

## Historical Context
Aristotle viewed axioms as "first principles" from which demonstrative sciences must start. Euclid's *Elements* demonstrated how complex geometric theorems could be derived from a small set of axioms and postulates.
