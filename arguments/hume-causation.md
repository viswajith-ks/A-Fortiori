# Hume's Critique of Causation

**Type:** Skeptical critique (empiricist analysis)  
**Target:** Necessity in [causal relationships](../concepts/metaphysics/causation.md)  
**Philosopher:** [David Hume](../thinkers/david-hume.md)

## The Challenge

Can we rationally justify our belief in necessary causal connections between events? Hume argues that causation is nothing more than constant conjunction plus psychological habit.

## Argument Structure

### Empirical Analysis

1. **Observational premise**: We never directly observe "causal necessity" or "causal power"
2. **Conjunction premise**: We only observe that A-type events are regularly followed by B-type events  
3. **Temporal premise**: We observe that causes precede effects in time
4. **Contiguity premise**: We observe spatial proximity between causes and effects

### Psychological Analysis

1. **Habit formation**: Repeated observation creates mental expectation
2. **Projection**: We project this internal expectation onto external world
3. **Illusion of necessity**: What feels like objective necessity is subjective habit

### Conclusion

Therefore, causal "necessity" is not a feature of reality but a product of human psychology.

## Hume's Positive Account

### Regularity Theory

- **Constant conjunction**: Causation = regular succession of similar events
- **No hidden powers**: No mysterious forces or necessary connections in nature
- **Phenomenological**: Focus on what we actually experience, not metaphysical speculation
- **Deflationary**: Causation reduced to observable patterns

### Custom and Habit

- **Natural belief**: We cannot help but expect future to resemble past
- **Non-rational**: This expectation not based on reasoning
- **Evolutionary adaptation**: Useful for survival even if not rationally justified
- **Inescapable**: Even Hume himself acts on causal expectations

### Two Definitions of Cause

**Philosophical definition**: "An object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second"

**Natural definition**: "An object followed by another, whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other"

## Implications for Knowledge

### [Problem of Induction](problem-of-induction.md)

- **Causal reasoning**: All empirical reasoning assumes uniformity of nature
- **No justification**: Cannot prove future will resemble past without circularity  
- **Habit not reason**: Inductive practices based on custom, not logic
- **Skeptical conclusion**: No rational foundation for causal knowledge

### Scientific Knowledge

- **Instrumentalism**: Scientific laws are useful descriptions, not discoveries of necessity
- **Predictive success**: Science works for practical purposes without metaphysical truth
- **Regularity focus**: Look for patterns, not hidden essences or powers
- **Fallibilism**: All causal claims revisable based on experience

## Historical Context

### Rationalist Background

- **Descartes**: Mechanical philosophy with strict causal necessity
- **Spinoza**: Everything follows with logical necessity from nature
- **Leibniz**: Principle of sufficient reason - everything has cause
- **Hume's innovation**: Empirical critique of rationalist confidence

### Empiricist Development

- **Locke**: Ideas come from experience, but retained causal realism
- **Berkeley**: Causation only exists between minds (occasionalism)
- **Hume's advance**: Consistent empiricism applied to causation itself

## Contemporary Responses

### Kantian Response

- **Transcendental argument**: Causation is necessary condition for unified experience
- **Category of understanding**: Mind structures experience causally
- **Synthetic a priori**: Causal principle knowable independently of particular experience
- **Phenomena vs noumena**: Causation applies to appearances, not things-in-themselves

### Neo-Humean Approaches

- **Lewis's counterfactuals**: A causes B if B wouldn't occur without A in closest possible worlds
- **Probabilistic causation**: Causes raise probability of effects
- **Causal models**: Directed graphs representing causal relationships
- **Interventionist theories**: Causes are variables that make difference when manipulated

### Causal Realism

- **Powers ontology**: Objects have intrinsic causal dispositions
- **Natural necessity**: Laws of nature govern causal relationships
- **Scientific realism**: Theoretical entities have real causal powers
- **Inference to best explanation**: Causal realism explains success of science

### Pragmatist Response

- **James and Dewey**: Causal concepts justified by practical success
- **Instrumentalism**: Causal theories as tools for prediction and control
- **Fallibilism**: Causal knowledge provisional and revisable
- **Naturalized epistemology**: Study causal reasoning as natural phenomenon

## Modern Developments

### Philosophy of Science

- **Causal explanation**: Role of causation in scientific explanation
- **Experimental method**: Controlled manipulation to identify causes
- **Causal inference**: Statistical methods for inferring causation from correlation
- **Mechanisms**: Focus on intermediate steps connecting causes and effects

### Cognitive Science

- **Causal cognition**: How humans learn and reason about causation
- **Development**: Children's acquisition of causal concepts
- **Cross-cultural**: Universal vs culturally variable aspects of causal thinking
- **Neuroscience**: Brain mechanisms underlying causal reasoning

### Artificial Intelligence

- **Causal networks**: Bayesian networks representing probabilistic causation
- **Machine learning**: Algorithms for discovering causal relationships in data
- **Counterfactual reasoning**: AI systems that can reason about alternative scenarios
- **Explainable AI**: Making algorithmic decisions causally interpretable

## Critiques of Hume

### Phenomenological Critique

- **Direct experience**: We do experience causal efficacy in action
- **Agent causation**: First-person experience of making things happen
- **Resistance**: Physical objects provide felt resistance to our efforts
- **Temporal experience**: Experience of temporal flow involves causal succession

### Conceptual Critique

- **Circularity**: Hume's own account relies on causal concepts (habit formation, projection)
- **Regularity problems**: Accidental regularities, spurious correlations
- **Single cases**: Individual causal judgments not based on regularities
- **Counterfactuals**: We judge causation by what would happen in different circumstances

### Scientific Realist Critique

- **Success of science**: Causal realism explains predictive and technological success
- **Unification**: Causal laws unify diverse phenomena
- **Novel predictions**: Theories postulating new causal relationships make successful predictions
- **Theoretical entities**: Unobservable causes (electrons, genes) prove scientifically fruitful

## Applications and Implications

### Legal and Moral Responsibility

- **Causal responsibility**: Legal liability often depends on causal contribution
- **Moral luck**: Consequences beyond agent's control affect moral evaluation
- **Negligence**: Standard of reasonable care in preventing harmful consequences
- **Criminal causation**: Requirement that actions cause prohibited consequences

### Medical and Health Sciences

- **Epidemiology**: Identifying causal factors in disease
- **Clinical trials**: Experimental methods for establishing treatment efficacy
- **Public health**: Interventions based on causal understanding
- **Evidence-based medicine**: Hierarchy of evidence for causal claims

### Economics and Social Science

- **Causal inference**: Identifying effects of policies and interventions
- **Natural experiments**: Exploiting quasi-random variation
- **Instrumental variables**: Using exogenous factors to identify causal effects
- **Regression discontinuity**: Causal inference at arbitrary thresholds
