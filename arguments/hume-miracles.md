# Hume's Argument Against Miracles

**Type:** Skeptical argument (critique of religious epistemology)

**Target:** Rational belief in [miracles](../concepts/philosophy-religion/miracles.md) ⚠️ Placeholder based on testimony

**Philosopher:** [David Hume](../thinkers/david-hume.md)

## The Challenge

Can testimony ever provide sufficient evidence to rationally believe in miracles? Hume argues that the evidence against miracles will always outweigh the evidence for them.

## Definition of Miracle

**Hume's Definition**: "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined."

## The Argument Structure

### Part I: The A Priori Argument

#### Premises

1. **Miracles violate [laws of nature](../concepts/metaphysics/laws-of-nature.md) ⚠️ Placeholder** by definition
2. **Laws of nature are established by uniform experience** - constant conjunction without exception
3. **Evidence for laws is maximal** - based on all human experience throughout history
4. **Evidence against miracle is evidence for law** - every instance of law operating normally

#### The Balance of Evidence

1. **Miracle claim vs universal experience** - testimony of few vs experience of many
2. **Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence** - more extraordinary, more evidence needed
3. **Evidence for miracles is testimonial** - depends on reliability of witnesses
4. **Evidence against is experiential** - direct experience of natural regularity

#### Conclusion

Therefore, evidence against any alleged miracle will always be stronger than evidence for it, making belief irrational.

### Part II: The A Posteriori Argument

Even if the a priori argument fails, examining actual miracle claims reveals additional problems:

#### Problems with Witnesses

- **Education and civilization**: Miracles reported mainly among "ignorant and barbarous nations"
- **Psychological factors**: "Passion of surprise and wonder" distorts judgment
- **Religious enthusiasm**: Strong religious commitment biases observation
- **Love of the marvelous**: Natural human tendency to believe extraordinary tales

#### Problems with Testimony

- **Insufficient witnesses**: Rarely multiple independent reliable witnesses
- **Interested parties**: Witnesses often have religious or social reasons to lie
- **Chain of transmission**: Stories change through retelling
- **Contemporary witnesses**: Lack of contemporary historically verified accounts

#### Competing Miracles

- **Different religions**: All claim miracles supporting their doctrines
- **Mutual cancellation**: Evidence for one religion's miracles counts against others'
- **No neutral criteria**: No way to adjudicate between competing miracle claims
- **Cultural relativism**: Miracle beliefs vary by culture and historical period

## Detailed Analysis

### The Epistemological Framework

**Empiricist foundation**: All knowledge comes from experience

- **Constant conjunction**: [Causation](../concepts/metaphysics/causation.md) based on regular succession
- **[Inductive reasoning](../concepts/epistemology/induction.md)**: Future expected to resemble past
- **Probability**: Beliefs proportioned to evidence
- **Testimony**: Derivative evidence based on others' experience

### The Probabilistic Argument

1. **Probability of testimony being true** depends on:
   - Reliability of witnesses
   - Number of independent witnesses
   - Their education and character
   - Possible motives for deception

2. **Probability of natural law being violated** approaches zero:
   - Based on universal uniform experience
   - Never observed single exception
   - Confirmed by all scientific investigation

3. **Rational belief**: Believe whichever has higher probability
4. **Result**: Natural law more probable than testimonial reliability

### Hume's Examples

#### Indian Prince and Ice

- Prince who never experienced freezing disbelieves reports of water turning solid
- Eventually convinced by multiple reliable witnesses and consistency of reports
- **Analogy**: But miracle claims lack such overwhelming testimonial evidence

#### Cardinal de Retz

- Admits he would not believe miracle even if he saw it himself
- Would assume hallucination or deception rather than law violation
- **Implication**: Even direct experience insufficient for miracle belief

#### Jansenist Miracles

- Contemporary miracle claims at tomb of Abbé Paris
- Many witnesses of allegedly supernatural healings
- **Hume's response**: Even these well-attested claims insufficient given extraordinary nature

## Responses and Criticisms

### Religious Responses

#### Paley's Response

- **God as lawmaker**: God can suspend laws God created
- **Evidential value**: Miracles serve as divine signature on revelation
- **Historical evidence**: Some miracle claims have strong historical support
- **Prior probability**: Theism raises prior probability of miracles

#### Swinburne's Bayesian Approach

- **Prior probability**: P(miracle) depends on P(theism)
- **Likelihood**: P(evidence|miracle) vs P(evidence|no miracle)
- **Posterior probability**: Use Bayes' theorem to calculate final probability
- **Result**: Strong historical evidence could overcome low prior probability

#### Lewis's Critique

- **Question-begging**: Hume assumes naturalistic worldview
- **Circular reasoning**: Uses naturalism to argue against supernaturalism
- **Historical possibility**: Some events might genuinely be unprecedented
- **Epistemic limitations**: Our experience limited - cannot establish universal laws

### Philosophical Criticisms

#### Definition Problems

- **Laws of nature**: What exactly are natural laws?
- **Violation vs suspension**: Different concepts of miracle
- **Repeatability**: Could miracles follow higher-order laws?
- **Statistical vs universal**: Are laws statistical or absolute?

#### Epistemological Problems

- **Testimony**: Can be very strong evidence in right circumstances
- **Multiple attestation**: Independent witnesses can provide strong evidence
- **Expert testimony**: Some witnesses more reliable than others
- **Background knowledge**: Need to consider total evidence, not just regularity

#### Historical Criticism

- **Anachronism**: Applies modern scientific worldview to pre-scientific periods
- **Cultural bias**: Dismisses non-Western sources of evidence
- **Selection bias**: Focuses on weak cases, ignores stronger candidates
- **Historical method**: Historians regularly accept unprecedented events

### Contemporary Developments

#### Bayesian Epistemology

- **Formal framework**: Precise calculation of probabilities
- **Prior probability**: P(miracle) depends on worldview
- **Likelihood ratios**: Evidence evaluated relative to hypotheses
- **Posterior probability**: Updated belief after considering evidence

#### Historical Jesus Studies

- **Resurrection**: Central miracle claim of Christianity
- **Historical methods**: Apply standard historical criteria
- **Multiple attestation**: Early and diverse sources
- **Embarrassment**: Some features unlikely to be invented

#### Philosophy of Science

- **Falsifiability**: Scientific laws must be falsifiable
- **Paradigm shifts**: Apparent law violations can lead to new science
- **Anomalies**: Unexplained phenomena common in science
- **Explanation**: Miracles as explanatory hypotheses
