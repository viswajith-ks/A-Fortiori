# The Problem of Induction

**Type:** Philosophical challenge (skeptical argument)  
**Target:** [Inductive reasoning](../concepts/epistemology/induction.md), [Epistemology](../concepts/epistemology/knowledge.md), [Science](../concepts/metaphysics/reality.md)  
**Philosopher:** [David Hume](../thinkers/david-hume.md), early empiricists

## What Is Induction?

Induction is reasoning from particular observations to general principles or predictions, fundamental to science and everyday life: e.g., "The sun has risen every day; therefore, it will rise tomorrow."

## Hume’s Challenge

Hume asked: On what rational basis do we infer general laws from past experience?  

- Our expectation that the future will resemble the past (the uniformity of nature) cannot be justified deductively—nor can it be proved by further induction without being circular.

## Argument Structure

1. Observational premises: “All observed swans are white.”
2. Inference: “Therefore, all swans are white.”
3. **Premise Problem:** The transition from observed cases to an unobserved generalization requires the assumption that nature is uniform—but this assumption cannot be justified by induction without begging the question.

## Classic Formulation

- Every inductive inference depends on the principle: “The future will resemble the past.”
- This principle itself cannot be established by induction or deduction.
- Thus, induction is **not rationally justified**—even though it is practically indispensable.

## Responses & Critiques

- **Reliabilism:** Induction is justified because it reliably leads to true beliefs.
- **Pragmatism:** Inductive reasoning is rational because it “works” practically.
- **Popper's Falsificationism:** Scientific knowledge progresses by falsifying hypotheses, not confirming them through induction.
- **Bayesian Epistemology:** Reframes induction as updating probabilities in light of evidence.
