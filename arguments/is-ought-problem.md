# Hume's Is-Ought Problem

**Type:** Metaethical challenge (critique of natural law ethics)  
**Target:** Deriving normative conclusions from descriptive premises  
**Philosopher:** [David Hume](../thinkers/david-hume.md)

## The Challenge
Can we legitimately derive statements about what we *ought* to do from statements about what *is* the case? Hume argues this inference is invalid and unjustified.

## Hume's Formulation

### *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-40)
"In every system of morality, which I have hitherto met with, I have always remarked, that the author proceeds for some time in the ordinary way of reasoning... when of a sudden I am surprised to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, *is*, and *is not*, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an *ought*, or an *ought not*."

### The Logical Gap
- **Descriptive premises**: Statements about facts, properties, natural laws
- **Normative conclusions**: Statements about duties, obligations, values
- **Missing link**: No logical bridge between "is" and "ought"
- **Invalid inference**: Normative conclusions don't follow from purely factual premises

## Argument Structure

### Formal Analysis
1. **Premises**: P₁, P₂, P₃... (all descriptive statements about facts)
2. **Conclusion**: Therefore, we ought to do X (normative statement)
3. **Logical gap**: Conclusion contains normative content not present in premises
4. **Invalid inference**: Normative conclusions require normative premises

### Missing Premise Problem
- **Hidden assumptions**: "Is-ought" arguments typically contain unstated normative premises
- **Example**: "Lying causes harm" + "We ought not cause harm" → "We ought not lie"
- **Buried normativity**: The moral work done by hidden normative assumption
- **Incomplete argument**: Making implicit premises explicit reveals normative assumptions

## Applications and Examples

### Natural Law Theory
- **Traditional approach**: Moral obligations derived from human nature or natural purposes
- **Aristotelian version**: Good life = fulfilling human essence/function  
- **Thomistic version**: Natural law accessible to reason, grounded in divine eternal law
- **Hume's critique**: No logical derivation from facts about nature to moral obligations

### Evolutionary Ethics
- **Sociobiological approach**: Moral behaviors explained by evolutionary fitness
- **Example**: Altruism increases inclusive fitness → We ought to be altruistic
- **Is-ought gap**: Survival value doesn't entail moral obligation
- **Naturalistic fallacy**: Confusing what is adaptive with what is good

### Social Contract Theory
- **Descriptive claim**: Humans need cooperation for mutual benefit
- **Normative conclusion**: Therefore, we ought to follow social contracts
- **Gap**: Why does need for cooperation create moral obligation?
- **Response**: Rational self-interest, not pure description

### Cultural Relativism
- **Descriptive premise**: Different cultures have different moral practices
- **Normative conclusion**: Therefore, we ought to be tolerant/relativistic
- **Problem**: Factual diversity doesn't entail normative relativism
- **Self-refutation**: Relativism itself makes universal normative claim

## Responses and Attempted Solutions

### Naturalistic Approaches

#### Neo-Aristotelian Virtue Ethics
- **Foot, MacIntyre**: Human flourishing provides objective standard
- **Thick concepts**: Concepts like "courage" are both descriptive and evaluative
- **Natural goodness**: Standards of goodness internal to living kinds
- **Response to Hume**: No sharp fact/value distinction in thick ethical concepts

#### Evolutionary Naturalism
- **Ruse, Alexander**: Moral beliefs shaped by evolutionary pressures
- **Debunking**: Evolution explains why we have moral beliefs, not whether they're true
- **Reformed naturalism**: Evolution makes our moral beliefs reliable
- **Persistence of gap**: Causal explanation doesn't provide justification

### Non-Naturalistic Approaches

#### Intuitionism
- **Moore, Ross**: Moral facts are non-natural facts knowable by intuition
- **Sui generis**: Moral properties irreducible to natural properties
- **Synthetic a priori**: Moral knowledge independent of empirical facts
- **Avoids is-ought**: Moral premises drawn from moral intuition, not natural facts

#### Expressivism
- **Ayer, Stevenson, Blackburn**: Moral judgments express attitudes, not beliefs about facts
- **Non-cognitive**: "Murder is wrong" expresses disapproval, doesn't state fact
- **No derivation needed**: Not trying to derive normative from descriptive
- **Quasi-realism**: Earn right to moral truth-talk through expressivist foundations

### Kantian Approaches

#### Constructivism
- **Rawls, Korsgaard**: Moral principles constructed through rational procedures
- **Procedural**: Focus on correct procedures rather than moral facts
- **Practical reason**: Normativity emerges from requirements of rational agency
- **Autonomy**: Moral agents legislate moral law for themselves

#### Transcendental Arguments
- **Strategy**: Show moral concepts necessary for rational agency or communication
- **Apel, Habermas**: Argumentation presupposes moral norms
- **Gewirth**: Action presupposes rights claims
- **Kantian response**: Normativity built into rational agency itself

### Contemporary Developments

#### Thick Concepts
- **Williams, Putnam**: Some concepts inherently both descriptive and evaluative
- **Examples**: Cruel, courageous, honest, rude
- **Entanglement thesis**: Fact and value intertwined in thick concepts
- **Challenge to gap**: No sharp distinction between is and ought

#### Moral Functionalism
- **Jackson, Pettit**: Moral properties are functional properties
- **Analytic descriptivism**: Moral concepts analytically tied to descriptive concepts
- **A priori connection**: Bridge between is and ought built into meaning of moral terms
- **Objection**: Implausible that moral concepts have analytic definitions

## Philosophical Implications

### Metaethical Significance
- **Fact/value distinction**: Fundamental difference between descriptive and normative
- **Autonomy of ethics**: Morality not reducible to non-moral facts
- **Moral epistemology**: How do we gain knowledge of moral facts?
- **Moral ontology**: Do moral facts exist? What is their nature?

### Methodology in Ethics
- **Pure practical reason**: Ethics as autonomous domain of reasoning
- **Reflective equilibrium**: Balancing principles and particular judgments
- **Wide reflective equilibrium**: Including empirical facts about human psychology
- **Moral psychology**: Role of empirical facts about moral motivation

### Applied Ethics
- **Bioethics**: Deriving policies from facts about suffering, autonomy, etc.
- **Environmental ethics**: Moving from ecological facts to environmental duties
- **Business ethics**: Inferring obligations from facts about market systems
- **Political philosophy**: Deriving just institutions from facts about human nature

## Contemporary Debates

### Neo-Humean Approaches
- **Blackburn**: Quasi-realist expressivism earns right to moral truth
- **Gibbard**: Norm-expressivism with sophisticated semantic treatment
- **Ridge**: Ecumenical expressivism accommodating moral knowledge
- **Challenge**: Explaining moral progress and disagreement

### Neo-Aristotelian Revival
- **McDowell**: Second nature bridges gap between nature and normativity
- **Thompson**: Natural goodness provides objective standards
- **Hursthouse**: Virtue ethics based on facts about human flourishing
- **Criticism**: Smuggling normative assumptions into "natural" facts

### Moral Realism
- **Boyd**: Moral facts are natural facts identified by mature moral theory
- **Sturgeon**: Moral explanations play genuine explanatory role
- **Enoch**: Robust realism with irreducible normative facts
- **Problem**: Still faces question of how we know moral facts

## Relationship to Other Problems

### Naturalistic Fallacy
- **Moore's critique**: Identifying good with natural properties commits fallacy
- **Open question argument**: Always meaningful to ask "But is it good?"
- **Connection**: Related to but distinct from is-ought problem
- **Difference**: Naturalistic fallacy about definition; is-ought about inference

### Fact/Value Distinction
- **Weber**: Science should be value-free
- **Logical positivism**: Only factual statements cognitively meaningful
- **Putnam's critique**: Fact/value distinction untenable
- **Contemporary**: Debate over objectivity in social science

### Moral Motivation
- **Internalism**: Moral judgments necessarily motivate
- **Externalism**: Moral judgments motivate only contingently
- **Humean psychology**: Beliefs alone cannot motivate action
- **Connection**: Relates to whether moral facts can be purely descriptive

## Significance for A Fortiori
The is-ought problem exemplifies careful logical analysis revealing hidden assumptions in apparently valid arguments. Hume's insight demonstrates the importance of examining the logical structure of reasoning - a principle central to systematic approach to philosophical arguments.

## Key Questions
- Can the is-ought gap ever be legitimately bridged?
- Are there thick concepts that are inherently both descriptive and normative?  
- Does the autonomy of ethics require complete independence from factual considerations?
- How should empirical facts about human nature inform moral philosophy?
